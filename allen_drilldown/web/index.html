Orientation tuning in mouse V1, one click at a time

Real Neuropixels data. This starts with the figure everyone publishes — the average response across all recorded neurons, one trace per grating direction — and lets you click down to the data it was computed from.

The population average is almost flat. That is not a bug and not a dead recording: three quarters of these neurons are strongly orientation-tuned, but they prefer different orientations, so averaging across them cancels the tuning out. You cannot see that from the top panel. You can see it immediately one level down.

    What you are looking at

    One probe from one session of the Allen Institute Visual Coding – Neuropixels dataset. The stimulus is drifting gratings: 8 directions × 5 temporal frequencies × 15 repeats. We pool across temporal frequency, so each direction has ~75 trials. Grey shading marks the 2 s the grating was on screen.

    The dots are somebody's opinion

    Every spike here is the output of a spike sorter (Kilosort2), not ground truth. A unit that looks beautifully tuned might be two cells merged; a silent one might be a real cell whose spikes were split across two clusters. The units shown are the ones that pass Allen's default quality control — quality = good, amplitude cutoff < 0.1, presence ratio > 0.95, ISI violations < 0.5. That filter is a scientific choice, and the population average at the top depends on it.

    Where the raw voltage comes from

    The bottom panel is genuine 30 kHz voltage from the probe, not a reconstruction. Allen's spike_band.dat for this probe is 217 GB, and their S3 bucket sends no CORS header, so a browser can neither stream nor read it. But the file is flat channel-interleaved int16, so a time window is contiguous bytes: 40 windows — one per direction × temporal frequency — were pulled with ranged GETs from Python, high-pass filtered at 300 Hz for display, common-average-referenced, quantised to int8 and stored next to this page as a 22 MB file. The page then fetches one 0.5 MB slice per trial with its own ranged GET, so nothing downloads until you ask for it. Rows marked ▸ in the trial raster have a snippet.

    Getting the two clocks to agree was the fiddly part. The probe samples and the NWB spike times live on different clocks, related through barcode pulses recorded on both; matching those gives a map good to a few tens of microseconds. As a check, every unit's spike-triggered average shows a sharp trough on its own peak channel — and it sits a constant 0.77 ms after the stored spike time, because Kilosort timestamps the start of its template window rather than the trough. The dots are shifted by that amount so they land on the waveforms.

    A synthetic-data version of the same drill-down lets you compare: there the dots really are ground truth, because the waveforms were put there on purpose. Code for both is in the Good Analysis Practice repository.
